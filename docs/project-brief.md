# Regulating the Feed

### A comparative study of enacted social media regulation across eight selected regulatory systems

*Independent research · 2026*

## Summary

Social media regulation has long addressed content and the collection and use of personal data. More recently, enacted laws and regulations have begun to reach **platform design and operation directly, including how users access services, how feeds and recommendation systems function, and how engagement features shape use**. *Regulating the Feed* examines that development across eight regulatory systems: the United States, European Union, United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, Brazil, China, and Indonesia. The study identified 34 qualifying enacted regulatory actions across the selected systems; Canada remained part of the comparative design but produced no qualifying action during the study period.

The study focuses on three areas of regulation: **Account and Service Access, Feeds and Recommendation Systems, and Engagement Design**. It compares the emerging U.S. state landscape with regulatory approaches outside the United States and tracks not only what governments regulate, but how they intervene and whether enacted requirements were legally operative at the study cutoff.

A central pattern is convergence on **platform design and operation as regulatory targets** without convergence on a common legal model. U.S. state actions in the sample more often use **Parental Controls**, while actions outside the United States more often use **Broader Safety Duties**. At the same time, the U.S. landscape is considerably more fragmented in operative status.

## Introduction

Individual statutes and regulatory systems reveal how particular governments are responding to concerns about social media, but they make broader patterns difficult to evaluate systematically. Doctrinal legal analysis remains necessary for determining the meaning, scope, and validity of individual legal instruments. It does not, by itself, quantify how frequently particular regulatory mechanisms appear across jurisdictions, how those mechanisms overlap, or whether enacted requirements are legally operative at the same point in time. A consistently coded dataset provides a complementary way to examine those questions across a selected legal landscape.

*Regulating the Feed* uses systematic empirical legal mapping to compare enacted requirements addressing three dimensions of social media design and operation: **Account and Service Access, Feeds and Recommendation Systems, and Engagement Design**. The study also distinguishes the form of intervention through five **regulatory approaches**: Direct Limits, User Choice and Control, Parental Controls, Warnings and Interruptions, and Broader Safety Duties. Target population and operative status are coded separately so that the population protected, the mechanism selected, and the legal status of an intervention remain analytically distinct.

The study asks: **How are selected regulatory systems using enacted law to regulate social media access, recommendation systems, and engagement design, and how does the approach emerging among U.S. states compare with approaches in the other sampled systems?** Its descriptive objectives are to map the areas of platform design and operation being regulated, compare regulatory approaches across the U.S. and non-U.S. groups, examine how youth protection is implemented, and document differences between enacted and operative regulation at a common cutoff. The study is descriptive and comparative rather than a test of a prespecified causal hypothesis. Explanatory propositions developed in the Discussion are therefore treated as interpretations or questions for further research rather than established causal findings.

## Methods

### Study design and comparative sample

The project is a **descriptive comparative empirical legal study** based on a purposive sample of eight regulatory systems: the United States, European Union, United Kingdom, Australia, Canada, Brazil, China, and Indonesia. The sample was designed to compare the developing U.S. state-level landscape with a broader set of non-U.S. regulatory approaches rather than to provide a globally representative census of social media regulation.

The study covers regulatory actions enacted or formally adopted from **January 1, 2018 through September 11, 2026**. Operative status was assessed as of **September 11, 2026**, providing a common point in time for the legal-status comparison. The final dataset contains **34 qualifying regulatory actions**, including **27 U.S. state actions** and **seven actions outside the United States**. Canada remained part of the comparative design although no enacted action met the inclusion criteria during the study period.

### Eligibility and unit of analysis

Within each selected system, enacted statutes, regulations, and binding subordinate legislation were screened for requirements directly addressing at least one of the three defined regulatory areas. Proposed legislation, voluntary codes, nonbinding guidance, policy proposals, and legal instruments that did not create a qualifying binding obligation were excluded. Instruments concerning online safety, children, privacy, or digital services were not included solely because they addressed those subjects; eligibility depended on the substance of the legal requirements.

The unit of analysis is a **regulatory action**. One material enacted regulatory intervention occupies one row in the dataset. Materially distinct actions from the same jurisdiction may therefore appear separately when they create separate qualifying interventions.

### Coding and operationalization

Each qualifying action was reviewed against a common coding framework. Specific legal requirements were recorded using binary variables, with `1` indicating that the enacted text contains a qualifying requirement under the project's coding rules and `0` indicating that it does not.

Specific requirements are aggregated into three nonexclusive **areas of regulation**:

- **Account and Service Access** identifies requirements governing whether and under what conditions users may access or maintain accounts on covered services.
- **Feeds and Recommendation Systems** identifies requirements governing personalization, recommender systems, feed choice, or duties associated with algorithmic recommendations.
- **Engagement Design** identifies requirements governing platform features or design practices that shape or prolong user engagement.

The dataset separately classifies five nonexclusive **regulatory approaches**: Direct Limits, User Choice and Control, Parental Controls, Warnings and Interruptions, and Broader Safety Duties. The distinction separates **what** part of a platform or user experience is regulated from **how** the law intervenes. Target population is coded independently as Minors, Mixed, or All Users.

### Operative status, sources, and analysis

Substantive coding reflects the enacted legal architecture even when a requirement was blocked, not yet effective, or otherwise affected by litigation. Operative status was assessed separately as **In Force, Partially Operative, Not Yet Effective, Blocked, or Invalidated/Repealed**. Primary legal sources were used wherever available to establish enacted requirements, with separate status sources used where necessary to verify injunctions, appellate decisions, implementation dates, or other developments affecting operation.

The quantitative analysis is descriptive. It reports action counts and percentages for the full dataset and, where relevant, separately for the U.S. and non-U.S. groups. Percentages use the number of actions in the stated group as their denominator. No inferential statistical test was performed, and the analysis does not estimate worldwide regulatory prevalence. Detailed eligibility rules, variable definitions, coding criteria, sourcing practices, and legal-status methodology are documented separately in the project [methodology](methodology.md) and [data dictionary](data-dictionary.md).

## Results

*All counts and percentages in this section are calculated from the project's coded dataset.*

### Sample composition and enactment timing

The dataset contains **34 regulatory actions**, including **27 U.S. state actions** and **seven qualifying actions outside the United States**. Canada was included in the comparative design but produced no qualifying action during the study period.

The actions are concentrated in the latter part of the study window. **32 of 34 actions (94.1%) were enacted in 2023 or later**, including **26 actions (76.5%) enacted from 2024 onward**.

### Areas of regulation

**Engagement Design is addressed by 27 of 34 regulatory actions (79.4%)**, compared with **16 actions (47.1%)** addressing Account and Service Access and **13 actions (38.2%)** addressing Feeds and Recommendation Systems.

The area indicators are nonexclusive, so one action may contribute to multiple counts. Nebraska's Age-Appropriate Online Design Code, for example, includes requirements concerning engagement features, feed choice, and time use, while New York's SAFE for Kids Act addresses both recommendation feeds and notifications.[^1]

### Regulatory approaches

The five regulatory approaches also overlap within individual actions.

**Direct Limits** appear in **21 of 27 U.S. actions (77.8%)** and **five of seven actions outside the United States (71.4%)**.

**User Choice and Control** appears in **three U.S. actions (11.1%)** and **three actions outside the United States (42.9%)**.

**Parental Controls** appear in **18 U.S. actions (66.7%)** and **three actions outside the United States (42.9%)**.

**Warnings and Interruptions** appear in **five U.S. actions (18.5%)** and none of the seven qualifying actions outside the United States.

**Broader Safety Duties** appear in **six U.S. actions (22.2%)** and **six of seven actions outside the United States (85.7%)**.

### Target population and youth account restrictions

**29 of 34 regulatory actions (85.3%) primarily target minors**, including **25 of 27 U.S. actions (92.6%)** and **four of seven qualifying actions outside the United States (57.1%)**.

Among the 29 youth-focused actions, **13 (44.8%) impose an account age restriction**. Parental Controls appear in 21 actions across the full dataset, and **11 of those 21 actions also impose an account age restriction**.

### Operative status

At the September 11, 2026 cutoff, the 27 U.S. actions were classified as **10 In Force, four Partially Operative, five Not Yet Effective, and eight Blocked**.

All **seven qualifying actions outside the United States were In Force** at the same cutoff.

The accompanying Tableau Public dashboard provides additional action-level and jurisdiction-level views of the coded results.

## Discussion

### Regulation is increasingly directed at platform design and functionality

The prevalence of Engagement Design, together with the regulation of Feeds and Recommendation Systems, indicates that the legal target in the sampled actions extends beyond content moderation, privacy, or account access. Governments are imposing requirements on systems and features that determine how content is recommended, how users are prompted to return or continue using a service, and how long or continuously users engage with a platform. Recommendation feeds, notifications, autoplay, continuous scrolling, time controls, warnings, and broader design-risk duties are therefore becoming direct objects of legal regulation.

The pattern is visible in both discrete feature rules and broader regulatory systems. The Digital Services Act requires very large platforms to assess systemic risks arising from the design or functioning of their services and expressly identifies changes to design, features, or functioning as possible mitigation measures. Ofcom's child safety framework similarly directs regulated services to consider recommender systems and functionalities that affect how much children use a service when assessing risk.[^5] These approaches differ in legal form, but both place product design and platform operation within the scope of regulatory responsibility.

The distinction has practical consequences for how the law must be analyzed and implemented. A legal obligation may attach to recommendation logic, interface behavior, default settings, or engagement features rather than to a discrete item of content. Compliance analysis therefore increasingly requires attention to how a service functions at the product level, not only to the rules governing what content may appear or how personal information is processed. The evidence does not establish that regulators agree on the causal relationship between particular design features and user harm, or that the interventions identified here are effective. Future research could extend the analysis from enacted legal design to implementation and outcomes, including whether different forms of design regulation materially change platform practices or user behavior.

### Regulatory responsibility is allocated differently across the sampled systems

The regulatory-approach results suggest that governments can regulate similar platform environments while assigning responsibility to different actors. U.S. state actions in the sample more often formalize parental authority and prescribe specific requirements for covered services, while several actions outside the United States place continuing safety, assessment, or mitigation duties directly on the provider. Direct Limits remain common in both groups, so the distinction is not between regulation and nonregulation. It concerns the legal mechanism through which responsibility is allocated.

Florida's Online Protections for Minors illustrates the first model by prohibiting account holding for children under 14 and conditioning account access for 14 and 15 year olds on parental consent. The European Union's Digital Services Act instead requires very large platforms to assess systemic risks and adopt reasonable, proportionate, and effective mitigation measures, which may include changes to service design, features, or functioning.[^2] Australia's minimum age law provides an important counterexample to any categorical geographic divide because it relies on a direct Account and Service Access restriction.[^2] The comparison therefore supports a distinction in the **mix of regulatory approaches**, not two uniform U.S. and non-U.S. models.

Differences in regulatory governance may help explain part of the pattern. The Digital Services Act operates through continuing supervision by the European Commission and national Digital Services Coordinators, while the United Kingdom's Online Safety Act gives Ofcom ongoing information-gathering and enforcement powers.[^7] Institutional arrangements of that kind can support duties requiring regulated firms to assess risks, document compliance, and adapt mitigation measures over time. Many U.S. state laws instead specify access conditions, feature restrictions, warnings, parental controls, or other obligations directly in statutory text.

The distinction also produces different compliance architectures. A discrete feature restriction can often be mapped to a particular product change, while a broader safety duty may require continuing risk assessment, documentation, supervision, and mitigation across a service. Similar regulatory concerns can therefore produce materially different legal obligations depending on how authority and responsibility are distributed. The project does not measure regulatory capacity, rulemaking authority, supervisory resources, or legislative intent, so governance cannot be established as the cause of the observed difference. A useful extension would code those institutional characteristics separately and test whether continuing supervisory authority is associated with greater reliance on Broader Safety Duties.

### Youth protection often changes the conditions of use rather than excluding minors

The youth-targeting results complicate an intuitive account of youth social media regulation as primarily a matter of minimum ages or account exclusion. Fewer than half of the youth-focused actions impose an account age restriction, and Parental Controls frequently appear without one. Youth regulation in the sample therefore concerns not only whether a minor may enter or remain on a service, but also the conditions under which use occurs after access is permitted.

California SB 976 combines parental controls with restrictions on personalized feeds and notifications. Nebraska's Age-Appropriate Online Design Code provides minors with controls over recommendation settings, engagement features, and time use. Brazil's ECA Digital combines age assurance with feed choice, time-use controls, and broader design duties.[^3] Across the sample, youth-focused regulation can address feeds, notifications, autoplay, continuous scrolling, defaults, warnings, time limits, parental tools, and broader safety duties.

The pattern is broader than minimum age rules or account bans. Some laws condition or restrict account creation, while others regulate how covered services may recommend content, encourage continued use, or structure parental and user control after access is permitted. **The more useful distinction is not simply whether a law keeps minors off a service before a specified age, but whether it regulates account access, the conditions of use, or both.**

### Constitutional and federal review are shaping the operative U.S. landscape

The U.S. status data show that operative status is not simply a question of whether an enacted law has reached its effective date. Blocked and Partially Operative actions include laws whose enforceability had been altered through litigation. Federal constitutional review and preemption are therefore materially affecting the gap between what states have enacted and what governs in practice.

Arkansas's 2023 Social Media Safety Act required age verification and parental consent before minors could hold covered accounts and was permanently enjoined after the federal court found First Amendment and vagueness problems. Act 900 of 2025 added requirements concerning addictive practices, recommendations, notifications, and parental controls and was preliminarily enjoined after the court found likely First Amendment violations in several provisions and a likely vagueness problem in its prohibition on addiction-inducing practices. Act 901 separately imposed liability involving harmful designs, algorithms, or features and was preliminarily enjoined after the court found NetChoice likely to succeed on its First Amendment claim and identified a likely vagueness problem in its principal design liability provision.[^4][^6] Across the three laws, constitutional scrutiny reached account access requirements, feature-based restrictions, and design or algorithm liability.

Utah's 2024 Minor Protection in Social Media Act addressed the conditions under which minors could use social media by requiring covered services to disable autoplay, continuous scrolling, and certain push notifications and to provide supervisory time limits and mandatory breaks. A federal court preliminarily enjoined the law after finding that NetChoice was substantially likely to succeed on its First Amendment challenge. The Arkansas and Utah cases therefore show First Amendment scrutiny reaching both restrictions on access and regulation directed at the design and conditions of social media use.[^4]

Texas HB 18 remained Partially Operative in the dataset: its age registration and parental control provisions remained operative, while its monitoring and filtering requirement remained enjoined. The Fifth Circuit held that requirement preempted by Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act. In this context, Section 230 generally protects online services from being held liable as the publisher or speaker of content created by third parties and prevents states from imposing inconsistent liability rules. The court concluded that the Texas requirement conflicted with those protections because it imposed liability based on whether covered services adequately monitored, screened, or restricted third-party content. Federal law therefore prevented that specific requirement from operating while other parts of HB 18 remained in effect.[^8] The Texas outcome complements the First Amendment cases by showing that different sources of federal law can constrain different state regulatory strategies and produce selective rather than all-or-nothing effects on operative status.

Taken together, the U.S. status data show that enacted statutory text alone does not describe the operative regulatory landscape. **Federal constitutional review, preemption, injunctions, and appellate decisions are helping determine which parts of state social media laws actually govern.** Arkansas and Utah suggest that First Amendment scrutiny may be one reason operative status varies even across laws using different regulatory strategies, while Texas shows that federal statutory law can narrow state regulation through preemption as well.

The contrast with the non-U.S. actions warrants further study but should not be attributed to constitutional or governmental structure alone. All seven qualifying actions outside the United States were In Force at the cutoff, but the sampled systems differ substantially in constitutional review, allocation of legislative authority, regulatory supervision, and enforcement. Future research could test the First Amendment hypothesis more directly by tracing whether judicial decisions are followed by changes in statutory drafting or regulatory approach, using legislative histories, amendments, and later enactments. A broader comparative extension could examine how different systems of constitutional review and allocation of lawmaking authority affect whether enacted social media requirements become and remain operative.

### Convergence in regulatory targets has not produced a common legal model

Taken together, the findings point to **convergence on the object of regulation without convergence on the method of regulation**. Governments in the sample address access rules, recommendation systems, engagement features, user controls, parental authority, and broader platform duties, but combine those mechanisms differently. The same concerns about social media use can therefore produce an account restriction, a chronological feed option, a parental control requirement, a warning, a feature prohibition, or a continuing systemic-risk duty.

The distinction matters because similar regulatory targets do not create equivalent legal systems. Different models allocate authority and responsibility among users, parents, platforms, regulators, and courts. In the United States, judicial review adds another layer by determining whether enacted state requirements remain operative. The emerging pattern is therefore convergence on **platform design and operation as legitimate subjects of legal regulation**, without a common legal model for governing them.

### Limitations and directions for further research

The most important limitation concerns the structure of the comparative sample. The dataset contains **27 U.S. regulatory actions and seven qualifying actions outside the United States**, so percentages for the two groups should not be treated as equally precise estimates of broader regulatory tendencies. With only seven actions in the latter group, a single action changes a percentage by approximately **14.3 percentage points**. The comparison is descriptive of the regulatory actions captured by the study rather than a statistical inference about U.S. law and regulation elsewhere.

The unit of analysis also affects the comparison. Each row represents a regulatory action rather than a jurisdiction: China contributes two qualifying actions, several systems contribute one, Canada contributes none, and the United States contributes 27 state actions. The study therefore does not give each of the eight regulatory systems equal analytical weight. It also compares U.S. state legislation enacted within a federal system with predominantly national or supranational legal instruments operating under different allocations of legislative, administrative, and enforcement authority. Differences in regulatory breadth, supervision, enforcement design, or exposure to litigation may therefore reflect institutional structure and regulatory administration as well as substantive policy choices.

Time adds another source of asymmetry. Most regulatory actions in the dataset are recent, and many U.S. statutes were enacted near the end of the study period. Some had not yet reached their effective dates, while others remained in active litigation. The finding that all seven qualifying actions outside the United States were In Force at the September 11, 2026 cutoff is accurate for the period studied, but it should not be interpreted as a permanent or purely geographic difference in legal stability.

The coding framework also compresses legally distinct instruments into common variables so that comparison is possible. A `1` for Broader Safety Duties, for example, identifies the presence of a qualifying duty but does not imply that duties created by different laws are equivalent in scope, enforceability, or practical burden. The project measures enacted legal architecture and operative status, not enforcement intensity, platform compliance, implementation costs, behavioral outcomes, or policy effectiveness. The purposive selection of eight systems also excludes legal interventions that fall outside the three tracked regulatory areas, so the absence of a qualifying action does not mean that a jurisdiction lacks other relevant laws governing digital platforms.

Several extensions follow directly from these limitations. Future work could expand the comparative sample, code institutional and supervisory characteristics separately, track action- and provision-level status longitudinally, and connect enacted requirements to implementation or platform response. Legislative histories and later statutory revisions could also be used to test whether constitutional decisions influence the design of subsequent U.S. legislation rather than merely its operative status.

## Conclusion

Across the 34 qualifying regulatory actions, the study identifies a common movement toward regulating **platform design and operation**, but not a common legal model. Engagement Design is the most frequently coded area, 29 actions primarily target minors, and youth regulation frequently operates through conditions of use rather than account exclusion alone. U.S. actions more often use Parental Controls, while the qualifying actions outside the United States more often use Broader Safety Duties. The U.S. sample also shows substantially more variation in operative status, with constitutional review and federal preemption affecting which enacted requirements govern in practice.

The broader empirical pattern is therefore **convergence on shared regulatory targets without a common legal model**. Continued action-level and provision-level tracking can show whether these combinations persist as the field develops, whether litigation changes the design of later statutes, how institutional structures shape regulatory approaches, and whether enacted design requirements ultimately alter platform practices or user experience.
